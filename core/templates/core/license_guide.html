License guide

A short tour of the licenses that show up in the project form. I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice, just plain-language notes to help you pick something reasonable. When in doubt, ask your institution's tech-transfer office or a colleague who's published open-source work before.

What a license actually does

A license tells other people what they're allowed to do with your work. Without one, the default in most countries is "all rights reserved," which means a careful reader can't legally reuse your code or designs even if you posted them publicly. Picking a license is how you grant permission.

Different kinds of work want different licenses. Code, hardware designs, documentation, and datasets each have their own conventions. The form lets you pick one license per project; if your project has, say, code and a paper draft and a CAD file, the right answer is usually to license each piece in its own repository and pick whichever one is the project's center of gravity here.

Code, permissive

Permissive licenses let people do nearly anything with your code, including using it in proprietary products, as long as they keep your copyright notice.

  • MIT — short, well understood, the safe default for most code.
  • BSD-3-Clause — similar to MIT with an extra clause that says nobody can use your name to endorse their derivative without permission.
  • Apache-2.0 — like MIT plus an explicit patent grant. A good pick when there might be patents in the neighborhood.

Code, copyleft

Copyleft licenses say: you can use this, but if you distribute a modified version, your modifications also have to be open under the same license. The intent is to keep derivatives in the commons.

  • AGPL-3.0 — strong copyleft that also reaches network/service deployments. OSPREY's recommended copyleft pick when the work is software that may run as a service.
  • GPL-3.0 — strong copyleft for applications. Anyone shipping a product built on top has to release their changes under GPL too.
  • LGPL-3.0 — softer copyleft, designed for libraries. Lets proprietary applications link to your library without having to open up.
  • MPL-2.0 — file-level copyleft. Modifications to MPL files stay open, but you can combine MPL files with proprietary files in the same project.

Open hardware

Hardware needs its own licenses because the things being licensed are schematics, board files, and mechanical drawings, not code. CERN's Open Hardware License family is the most common pick.

  • CERN-OHL-S-2.0 — strongly reciprocal. Anyone who makes a derivative design has to release it under the same license.
  • CERN-OHL-W-2.0 — weakly reciprocal. Friendlier to mixed open and proprietary hardware.
  • CERN-OHL-P-2.0 — permissive. Closest hardware analog to MIT.
  • TAPR-OHL-1.0 — older copyleft hardware license.

Content and data

For papers, posters, figures, datasets, and documentation, the Creative Commons family is the standard.

  • CC-BY-4.0 — anyone can reuse and adapt as long as they credit you. The closest CC analog to MIT.
  • CC-BY-SA-4.0 — attribution plus share-alike. Derivatives must use the same license.
  • CC0-1.0 — public-domain dedication. No attribution required, no strings attached.

Public-domain code

The Unlicense dedicates code to the public domain. It's a fine pick for tiny utilities, but for anything substantial, MIT or CC0 are usually safer because their wording has held up better in international courts.

Other

If none of the above fit, pick "Other" and write the SPDX identifier or a short label. Examples: dual licensing, a custom institutional license, a license that isn't on this list yet. If you find yourself reaching for "Other" often, let me know and I'll add the license to the dropdown.
